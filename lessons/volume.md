# GED Math Lesson: Volume

## Goal

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

* understand what volume measures;
* find the volume of cubes and rectangular prisms;
* find the volume of cylinders, cones, and spheres;
* identify the correct dimensions for volume formulas;
* solve GED-style volume problems.

---

# 1. What Is Volume?

**Volume** is the amount of space inside a three-dimensional object.

Think of volume as:

* the amount of water a container can hold;
* the amount of space inside a box;
* the amount of material needed to fill an object.

Volume is measured in **cubic units**.

Examples:

$$
\text{in}^3,\quad \text{ft}^3,\quad \text{cm}^3,\quad \text{m}^3
$$

---

# 2. Volume vs. Surface Area

These concepts are different.

### Volume

Measures the **space inside** an object.

Example:

> How much water can a container hold?

### Surface Area

Measures the **outside** of an object.

Example:

> How much wrapping paper is needed to cover a box?

### Memory Trick

**Fill the inside → Volume**

**Cover the outside → Surface Area**

---

# 3. Volume of a Cube

A cube has six equal square faces.

Since all three dimensions are equal, the volume is:

$$
V=s^3
$$

where $s$ is the side length.

This is the same as:

$$
V=s\cdot s\cdot s
$$

### Example

A cube has a side length of $5$ cm.

$$
V=s^3
$$

$$
V=5^3
$$

$$
V=5\cdot5\cdot5
$$

$$
V=125
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{125\text{ cm}^3}
$$

---

# 4. Volume of a Rectangular Prism

A rectangular prism has:

* length;
* width;
* height.

The formula is:

$$
V=lwh
$$

where:

* $l$ = length;
* $w$ = width;
* $h$ = height.

### Example

A box has:

* length = $8$ ft;
* width = $5$ ft;
* height = $4$ ft.

$$
V=lwh
$$

$$
V=(8)(5)(4)
$$

$$
V=160
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{160\text{ ft}^3}
$$

---

# 5. Volume of a Prism

The volume of any prism can be found using:

$$
V=Bh
$$

where:

* $B$ = area of the base;
* $h$ = height of the prism.

For a rectangular prism:

$$
B=lw
$$

Therefore:

$$
V=(lw)h
$$

which gives:

$$
V=lwh
$$

### Important

The **base area** is not always the same as the height.

First find the area of the base, then multiply by the height.

---

# 6. Volume of a Cylinder

A cylinder has:

* a circular base;
* a height.

The volume formula is:

$$
V=\pi r^2h
$$

where:

* $r$ = radius of the circular base;
* $h$ = height.

The area of the circular base is:

$$
\pi r^2
$$

Then multiply by the height:

$$
V=(\pi r^2)h
$$

### Example

A cylinder has:

* radius = $3$ in;
* height = $8$ in.

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=\pi r^2h
$$

$$
V=(3.14)(3)^2(8)
$$

$$
V=(3.14)(9)(8)
$$

$$
V=226.08
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{226.08\text{ in}^3}
$$

---

# 7. Volume of a Cone

A cone has:

* a circular base;
* a height.

The formula is:

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h
$$

where:

* $r$ = radius;
* $h$ = vertical height.

### Important

For the volume of a cone, use the **vertical height**, not the slant height.

### Example

A cone has:

* radius = $4$ cm;
* height = $9$ cm.

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}(3.14)(4)^2(9)
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}(3.14)(16)(9)
$$

$$
V=150.72
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{150.72\text{ cm}^3}
$$

---

# 8. Volume of a Sphere

A sphere is a perfectly round three-dimensional object.

Its volume is:

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
$$

where $r$ is the radius.

### Example

A sphere has a radius of $3$ m.

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
$$

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}(3.14)(3)^3
$$

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}(3.14)(27)
$$

$$
V=113.04
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{113.04\text{ m}^3}
$$

---

# 9. Volume of a Pyramid

A pyramid has:

* a base;
* a height.

The volume formula is:

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}Bh
$$

where:

* $B$ = area of the base;
* $h$ = vertical height.

### Example

A square pyramid has:

* a base side length of $6$ ft;
* a height of $10$ ft.

First find the area of the base:

$$
B=s^2
$$

$$
B=6^2=36
$$

Now use the volume formula:

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}Bh
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}(36)(10)
$$

$$
V=120
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{120\text{ ft}^3}
$$

---

# 10. Finding Missing Dimensions

Sometimes a GED problem gives you the volume and asks you to find a missing dimension.

### Example

A rectangular box has:

* volume = $240$ in³;
* length = $10$ in;
* width = $6$ in.

Find the height.

Start with:

$$
V=lwh
$$

Substitute the known values:

$$
240=(10)(6)(h)
$$

$$
240=60h
$$

Divide both sides by $60$:

$$
h=4
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{4\text{ in}}
$$

# 11. GED-Style Example: Rectangular Prism

A storage box has a length of $12$ inches, a width of $8$ inches, and a height of $5$ inches.

What is the volume of the box?

Use:

$$
V=lwh
$$

$$
V=(12)(8)(5)
$$

$$
V=480
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{480\text{ in}^3}
$$

# 12. GED-Style Example: Cylinder

A cylindrical container has:

* radius = $5$ cm;
* height = $12$ cm.

Find the volume.

$$
V=\pi r^2h
$$

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=(3.14)(5)^2(12)
$$

$$
V=(3.14)(25)(12)
$$

$$
V=942
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{942\text{ cm}^3}
$$

---

# 13. GED-Style Example: Cone

A cone has:

* radius = $6$ ft;
* height = $8$ ft.

Find the volume.

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h
$$

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}(3.14)(6)^2(8)
$$

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}(3.14)(36)(8)
$$

$$
V=301.44
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{301.44\text{ ft}^3}
$$

---

# 14. GED-Style Example: Sphere

A spherical ball has a radius of $7$ cm.

Find its volume.

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
$$

Using:

$$
\pi\approx3.14
$$

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}(3.14)(7)^3
$$

$$
V=\frac{4}{3}(3.14)(343)
$$

$$
V\approx1436.03
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1436.03\text{ cm}^3}
$$

# Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Using Square Units

Volume must use **cubic units**.

Correct:

$$
\text{cm}^3
$$

Incorrect:

$$
\text{cm}^2
$$

---

### Mistake 2: Forgetting the Cube

For a cube:

$$
V=s^3
$$

not:

$$
V=6s^2
$$

The formula:

$$
6s^2
$$

is for the **surface area** of a cube.

---

### Mistake 3: Forgetting the $\frac{1}{3}$

The volume of a cone is:

$$
V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h
$$

The volume of a cylinder is:

$$
V=\pi r^2h
$$

A cone has one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same radius and height.

---

### Mistake 4: Using Slant Height for a Cone

For cone volume, use the **vertical height**.

The slant height is used for some surface-area calculations.

---

### Mistake 5: Confusing Radius and Diameter

Remember:

$$
d=2r
$$

Therefore:

$$
r=\frac{d}{2}
$$

If a problem gives you the diameter, divide it by $2$ before using the formula.

## Memory Trick

**Volume = Fill the Inside**

Ask yourself:

> What space is inside the object?

Then identify the correct shape, choose the formula, substitute the dimensions, and make sure your answer is in **cubic units**.
