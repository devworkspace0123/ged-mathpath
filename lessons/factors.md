# GED Math Lesson: Factors, Multiples, GCF, and LCM

## Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

* Identify the factors of a number.
* Use multiplication and division to check whether a number is a factor.
* Find factor pairs.
* Recognize prime and composite numbers.
* Use divisibility rules to find factors quickly.
* Find the prime factorization of a number.
* List multiples of a number.
* Find common factors.
* Find the **Greatest Common Factor (GCF)**.
* Find common multiples.
* Find the **Least Common Multiple (LCM)**.
* Understand the difference between GCF and LCM.
* Choose whether GCF or LCM is appropriate for a word problem.
* Apply GCF and LCM to GED-style problems.

---

# 1. Factors

A **factor** is a whole number that divides another number evenly.

In other words, when you divide a number by one of its factors, the remainder is zero.

For example:

$$
12 \div 3 = 4
$$

There is no remainder, so 3 is a factor of 12.

We can also see this using multiplication:

$$
3 \times 4 = 12
$$

Therefore, both 3 and 4 are factors of 12.

### Important Idea

If:

$$
a \times b = n
$$

then both $a$ and $b$ are factors of $n$.

For example:

$$
5 \times 6 = 30
$$

Therefore:

* 5 is a factor of 30.
* 6 is a factor of 30.

---

# 2. Factor Pairs

A **factor pair** is a pair of whole numbers that multiply to produce a particular number.

For example, the factor pairs of 12 are:

$$
1 \times 12 = 12
$$

$$
2 \times 6 = 12
$$

$$
3 \times 4 = 12
$$

Therefore, the factor pairs are:

$$
(1,12),\ (2,6),\ (3,4)
$$

Notice that once we reach $3 \times 4$, we don't need to continue.

Why?

Because the pairs begin repeating in reverse:

$$
4 \times 3
$$

$$
6 \times 2
$$

$$
12 \times 1
$$

We have already found those factors.

---

# 3. Finding All Factors of a Number

Let's find all the factors of 24.

Start with 1:

$$
1 \times 24 = 24
$$

Try 2:

$$
2 \times 12 = 24
$$

Try 3:

$$
3 \times 8 = 24
$$

Try 4:

$$
4 \times 6 = 24
$$

Now the factor pairs are:

$$
(1,24),\ (2,12),\ (3,8),\ (4,6)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24}
$$

are all the factors of 24.

---

# 4. A Quick Way to Check a Factor

Suppose you want to know whether 7 is a factor of 42.

Divide:

$$
42 \div 7 = 6
$$

Because the result is a whole number, 7 is a factor of 42.

Now suppose we want to know whether 8 is a factor of 42.

$$
42 \div 8 = 5.25
$$

The result is not a whole number.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{8\text{ is not a factor of }42}
$$

### Remember

A factor must divide the number **evenly**.

---

# 5. Divisibility

Sometimes you don't need to perform the entire division to determine whether a number is a factor.

There are useful **divisibility rules**.

## Divisibility by 2

A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is:

$$
0,\ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\text{ or }8
$$

Examples:

$$
24,\ 36,\ 108,\ 250
$$

are all divisible by 2.

But:

$$
15
$$

is not divisible by 2.

---

## Divisibility by 5

A number is divisible by 5 if its last digit is:

$$
0\text{ or }5
$$

Examples:

$$
25,\ 40,\ 75,\ 120
$$

---

## Divisibility by 10

A number is divisible by 10 if its last digit is 0.

Examples:

$$
30,\ 120,\ 450
$$

---

## Divisibility by 3

A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.

For example:

$$
123
$$

Add the digits:

$$
1+2+3=6
$$

Since 6 is divisible by 3, 123 is divisible by 3.

Therefore:

$$
123\div3=41
$$

---

## Divisibility by 9

A number is divisible by 9 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 9.

For example:

$$
729
$$

Add the digits:

$$
7+2+9=18
$$

Since:

$$
18\div9=2
$$

729 is divisible by 9.

---

## Divisibility by 4

A number is divisible by 4 if its last two digits form a number divisible by 4.

For example:

$$
316
$$

Look at the last two digits:

$$
16
$$

Since:

$$
16\div4=4
$$

316 is divisible by 4.

---

# 6. Prime and Composite Numbers

A **prime number** is a whole number greater than 1 that has exactly two positive factors:

$$
1
$$

and itself.

For example, 7 has only:

$$
1\times7=7
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{7\text{ is prime}}
$$

Other prime numbers include:

$$
2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 11,\ 13,\ 17,\ 19,\ldots
$$

---

## Composite Numbers

A **composite number** has more than two positive factors.

For example, 12 has:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12
$$

Therefore, 12 is composite.

### Important Exception

The number 1 is **neither prime nor composite**.

It has only one positive factor:

$$
1
$$

---

# 7. Prime Factorization

A **prime factorization** expresses a number as a product of prime numbers.

Let's find the prime factorization of 60.

Start by dividing by the smallest prime possible:

$$
60=2\times30
$$

Continue:

$$
30=2\times15
$$

Then:

$$
15=3\times5
$$

Therefore:

$$
60=2\times2\times3\times5
$$

Using exponents:

$$
\boxed{60=2^2\times3\times5}
$$

This is the prime factorization of 60.

---

# 8. Factor Trees

A factor tree is another way to find prime factorization.

For 36:

$$
36=6\times6
$$

Then factor each 6:

$$
6=2\times3
$$

So:

$$
36=(2\times3)(2\times3)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{36=2^2\times3^2}
$$

The important thing is that you continue factoring until **every factor is prime**.

---

# 9. Multiples

Factors and multiples are related, but they are not the same thing.

A **multiple** is the result of multiplying a number by a whole number.

For example, the multiples of 4 are:

$$
4\times1=4
$$

$$
4\times2=8
$$

$$
4\times3=12
$$

$$
4\times4=16
$$

$$
4\times5=20
$$

So the multiples of 4 begin:

$$
\boxed{4,8,12,16,20,24,28,\ldots}
$$

The three dots mean the list continues forever.

---

# 10. Factors vs. Multiples

This distinction is extremely important.

### Factors

Factors **divide into** a number evenly.

For example:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12
$$

are factors of 12.

There are a finite number of factors.

### Multiples

Multiples are created by **multiplying** a number.

The multiples of 12 are:

$$
12,24,36,48,60,\ldots
$$

There are infinitely many multiples.

### Think of It This Way

**Factors go into a number.**

**Multiples come from a number.**

---

# 11. Common Factors

A **common factor** is a factor shared by two or more numbers.

Let's find the common factors of 12 and 18.

Factors of 12:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12
$$

Factors of 18:

$$
1,2,3,6,9,18
$$

The factors appearing in both lists are:

$$
\boxed{1,2,3,6}
$$

These are the common factors.

---

# 12. Greatest Common Factor (GCF)

The **Greatest Common Factor**, or **GCF**, is the largest factor that two or more numbers have in common.

From our previous example:

Common factors of 12 and 18:

$$
1,2,3,6
$$

The greatest one is:

$$
\boxed{6}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6}
$$

---

# 13. Finding the GCF by Listing Factors

Let's find the GCF of 24 and 36.

Factors of 24:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24
$$

Factors of 36:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,9,12,18,36
$$

Common factors:

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12
$$

The greatest is 12.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{GCF}(24,36)=12}
$$

---

# 14. Finding the GCF Using Prime Factorization

For larger numbers, prime factorization can make finding the GCF easier.

Find the GCF of 36 and 48.

First find their prime factorizations:

$$
36=2^2\times3^2
$$

and:

$$
48=2^4\times3
$$

Look for prime factors that appear in **both** numbers.

For 2:

$$
2^2
$$

is the smaller power.

For 3:

$$
3^1
$$

is the smaller power.

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(36,48)
=
2^2\times3
$$

$$
=4\times3
$$

$$
\boxed{12}
$$

---

# 15. When Should You Use the GCF?

The GCF is especially useful when you want to **split or group things into the largest possible equal groups**.

For example:

You have 24 apples and 36 oranges.

You want to create the greatest possible number of identical groups, with no fruit left over.

The number of groups must divide both 24 and 36.

Therefore, we find:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(24,36)=12
$$

So we can make:

$$
\boxed{12\text{ identical groups}}
$$

Each group receives:

$$
24\div12=2
$$

apples and:

$$
36\div12=3
$$

oranges.

---

# 16. Common Multiples

A **common multiple** is a number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

Let's find the common multiples of 4 and 6.

Multiples of 4:

$$
4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,36,\ldots
$$

Multiples of 6:

$$
6,12,18,24,30,36,\ldots
$$

Numbers appearing in both lists include:

$$
12,24,36,\ldots
$$

These are common multiples.

---

# 17. Least Common Multiple (LCM)

The **Least Common Multiple**, or **LCM**, is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

From the previous example, the common multiples of 4 and 6 include:

$$
12,24,36,\ldots
$$

The smallest is:

$$
\boxed{12}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{LCM}(4,6)=12}
$$

---

# 18. Finding the LCM by Listing Multiples

Let's find the LCM of 8 and 12.

Multiples of 8:

$$
8,16,24,32,40,48,\ldots
$$

Multiples of 12:

$$
12,24,36,48,\ldots
$$

The first common multiple is:

$$
24
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{LCM}(8,12)=24}
$$

---

# 19. Finding the LCM Using Prime Factorization

For larger numbers, prime factorization is often more efficient.

Find the LCM of 18 and 24.

First factor both numbers:

$$
18=2\times3^2
$$

$$
24=2^3\times3
$$

For the LCM, we take **every prime factor that appears**, using the greatest power of each.

For 2, the greatest power is:

$$
2^3
$$

For 3, the greatest power is:

$$
3^2
$$

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(18,24)
=
2^3\times3^2
$$

$$
=8\times9
$$

$$
\boxed{72}
$$

---

# 20. GCF vs. LCM

This is one of the most important distinctions to remember.

| GCF                                                | LCM                                                  |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Greatest Common Factor                             | Least Common Multiple                                |
| Finds the largest shared factor                    | Finds the smallest shared multiple                   |
| Usually involves dividing/grouping                 | Usually involves repeating cycles/groups             |
| Result is less than or equal to the smaller number | Result is greater than or equal to the larger number |

For example:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6
$$

while:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(12,18)=36
$$

### A Useful Memory Trick

**GCF → "What is the biggest thing that can divide both?"**

**LCM → "What is the smallest number both can reach?"**

---

# 21. When Should You Use GCF or LCM?

Look at what the problem is asking you to do.

### Think GCF When:

* You are dividing things into equal groups.
* You want the largest possible equal groups.
* You are simplifying fractions.
* You are splitting quantities evenly.

### Think LCM When:

* Things repeat at different intervals.
* You need to find when two events happen together.
* You need a common denominator for fractions.
* You are looking for the smallest number divisible by multiple numbers.

---

# 22. GCF and Simplifying Fractions

The GCF is very useful when simplifying fractions.

Consider:

$$
\frac{18}{24}
$$

We want to simplify it.

Find the GCF of 18 and 24:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(18,24)=6
$$

Divide both numerator and denominator by 6:

$$
\frac{18\div6}{24\div6}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{18}{24}=\frac{3}{4}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{\frac{18}{24}=\frac34}
$$

The GCF helps us reduce the fraction in one step.

---

# 23. LCM and Adding Fractions

The LCM can help us find a common denominator.

Consider:

$$
\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{8}
$$

The denominators are 6 and 8.

Find their LCM:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(6,8)=24
$$

So 24 is a common denominator.

Convert each fraction:

$$
\frac{1}{6}=\frac{4}{24}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{1}{8}=\frac{3}{24}
$$

Now add:

$$
\frac{4}{24}+\frac{3}{24}
=
\frac{7}{24}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{8}=\frac{7}{24}}
$$

---

# 24. GED-Style Example: GCF

A teacher has 48 pencils and 60 erasers.

She wants to make identical supply packages using all the items.

What is the greatest number of packages she can make?

We need the greatest number that divides both 48 and 60.

Therefore, we need the GCF.

Prime factorization:

$$
48=2^4\times3
$$

$$
60=2^2\times3\times5
$$

Take the common prime factors with the smallest powers:

$$
2^2\times3
$$

$$
=4\times3
$$

$$
=12
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{12}
$$

packages can be made.

Each package contains:

$$
48\div12=4
$$

pencils and:

$$
60\div12=5
$$

erasers.

---

# 25. GED-Style Example: LCM

One bus arrives every 12 minutes.

Another bus arrives every 18 minutes.

If both buses arrive at the same time now, after how many minutes will they arrive together again?

We need the smallest number that is divisible by both 12 and 18.

Therefore, we need the LCM.

Prime factorizations:

$$
12=2^2\times3
$$

$$
18=2\times3^2
$$

Take the greatest power of every prime:

$$
2^2\times3^2
$$

$$
=4\times9
$$

$$
=36
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{36\text{ minutes}}
$$

The buses will arrive together again after 36 minutes.

---

# 26. A Useful Relationship Between GCF and LCM

For two positive integers $a$ and $b$, there is an important relationship:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{GCF}(a,b)\times\operatorname{LCM}(a,b)=a\times b}
$$

For example, consider 12 and 18.

We know:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6
$$

and:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(12,18)=36
$$

Multiply:

$$
6\times36=216
$$

Now multiply the original numbers:

$$
12\times18=216
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{6\times36=12\times18}
$$

This relationship can sometimes help you find the LCM when you already know the GCF.

---

# 27. Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Confusing Factors and Multiples

Factors of 6:

$$
1,2,3,6
$$

Multiples of 6:

$$
6,12,18,24,30,\ldots
$$

Factors stop.

Multiples continue forever.

---

### Mistake 2: Thinking 1 Is Prime

1 is **not prime**.

A prime number must have exactly two positive factors.

1 has only one:

$$
1
$$

---

### Mistake 3: Using the Largest Power for the GCF

Suppose:

$$
A=2^4\times3
$$

and:

$$
B=2^2\times3^2
$$

For the GCF, use the **smallest powers**:

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(A,B)
=
2^2\times3
$$

For the LCM, use the **largest powers**:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(A,B)
=
2^4\times3^2
$$

Remember:

**GCF → smallest shared powers**

**LCM → largest powers**

---

# 28. Summary

A **factor** divides a number evenly.

$$
3\times4=12
$$

so 3 and 4 are factors of 12.

A **multiple** is produced by multiplying a number by a whole number.

$$
4,8,12,16,20,\ldots
$$

are multiples of 4.

A **prime number** has exactly two positive factors:

$$
1\text{ and itself}
$$

The **GCF** is the greatest factor shared by two or more numbers.

The **LCM** is the smallest positive multiple shared by two or more numbers.

### Remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{GCF = greatest shared factor}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{LCM = least shared multiple}}
$$

And for prime factorization:

**GCF → use the smallest powers.**

**LCM → use the largest powers.**
